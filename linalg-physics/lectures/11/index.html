The central thread

The dual space V∗=L(V,F)V^*=\Lin(V,\F)V∗=L(V,F) collects the linear functionals on VVV; a basis eie_iei​ of VVV gives a dual basis eie^iei with ei(ej)=δjie^i(e_j)=\delta^i_jei(ej​)=δji​, and φ(v)=φivi\varphi(v)=\varphi_i v^iφ(v)=φi​vi. In a finite inner-product space Riesz identifies V∗V^*V∗ with VVV conjugate-linearly over C\CC (bras are dual vectors), while the double-dual map V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ is canonical and linear. A map T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W has a dual T′ ⁣:W∗→V∗T'\colon W^*\to V^*T′:W∗→V∗, T′φ=φ∘TT'\varphi=\varphi\circ TT′φ=φ∘T, whose matrix is the transpose of TTT's, with null⁡T′=(im⁡T)0\nul T'=(\rng T)^0nullT′=(imT)0. Bilinear and multilinear forms package several vector inputs into a scalar—the determinant being the alternating nnn-linear form. Vector components viv^ivi are contravariant, covector components φi\varphi_iφi​ covariant, summed by Einstein's convention and related through the metric gijg_{ij}gij​—the bookkeeping that tensors (Lecture 12) formalize.

Learn the argument

Let vectors act as measurements

Dual vectors are linear measurements. Finite Riesz, the canonical double dual, pullbacks, annihilators, and covariance each encode a different kind of duality.

proposition
Finite Riesz comparison

Let VVV be a finite-dimensional inner-product space over F\FF. Every algebraic functional φ∈V∗\varphi\in V^*φ∈V∗ has a unique representative u∈Vu\in Vu∈V with φ(v)=⟨u,v⟩\varphi(v)=\inner u vφ(v)=⟨u,v⟩ for all vvv. The map R ⁣:u↦⟨u, ⋅ ⟩R\colon u\mapsto\inner u{\,\cdot\,}R:u↦⟨u,⋅⟩ is linear over R\RR and conjugate-linear over C\CC; equivalently, in the complex case it is a linear isomorphism V‾→V∗\overline V\to V^*V→V∗ from the conjugate vector space.

The double dual

For finite-dimensional VVV, the map V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ sending vvv to the functional φ↦φ(v)\varphi\mapsto\varphi(v)φ↦φ(v) is a canonical isomorphism (no basis required).

definition
Annihilator

The annihilator of a subspace U⊆VU\subseteq VU⊆V is U0={φ∈V∗:φ(u)=0 for all u∈U}U^0=\set{\varphi\in V^*:\varphi(u)=0\text{ for all }u\in U}U0={φ∈V∗:φ(u)=0 for all u∈U}, a subspace of V∗V^*V∗. Algebraically U0≅(V/U)∗U^0\iso(V/U)^*U0≅(V/U)∗; if VVV is finite-dimensional, this gives dim⁡U+dim⁡U0=dim⁡V\dim U+\dim U^0=\dim VdimU+dimU0=dimV.

proposition
Dual maps, annihilators, and rank

Let T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W. Then null⁡T′=(im⁡T)0\nul T'=(\rng T)^0nullT′=(imT)0 in arbitrary algebraic spaces. If V,WV,WV,W are finite-dimensional, then rank⁡T′=rank⁡T,im⁡T′=(null⁡T)0.\rank T'=\rank T, \qquad \rng T'=(\nul T)^0.rankT′=rankT,imT′=(nullT)0. Consequently a finite matrix and its transpose have the same rank.

definition
Multilinear form

A kkk-linear form is a map V×⋯×V→FV\times\cdots\times V\to\FV×⋯×V→F (kkk factors) linear in each slot separately. The determinant is the unique alternating nnn-linear form on Fn\F^nFn with det⁡(e1,…,en)=1\det(e_1,\dots,e_n)=1det(e1​,…,en​)=1 (see the determinant facts recalled in Lecture 5; the referenced example below verifies the 2×22\times22×2 case).

“A vector and a covector have the same transformation law.”

Reveal the test

If [v]′=P−1[v][v]'=P^{-1}[v][v]′=P−1[v], then a fixed row covector must satisfy [ϕ]′=[ϕ]P[\phi]'=[\phi]P[ϕ]′=[ϕ]P, so [ϕ]′[v]′=[ϕ][v][\phi]'[v]'=[\phi][v][ϕ]′[v]′=[ϕ][v]. This component law is distinct from the inverse-matrix construction of the dual-basis elements.
