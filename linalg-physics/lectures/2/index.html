↳From spanning lists to coordinatesMotivation, scope, and the finite-dimensional setting

Last lecture a vector space was a set equipped with addition and scalar multiplication satisfying the vector-space axioms; a subset of an ambient vector space was a subspace exactly when it was closed under linear combinations. Today we turn linear combinations into the two most useful concepts in the subject. A basis is a minimal list of vectors from which every other vector is built in exactly one way; it is the abstract version of a coordinate system. The number of vectors in a basis is the dimension, the right notion of “size” for a vector space.

The pay-off is concrete. Once we fix a basis, every abstract vector—a polynomial, a matrix, a quantum state—becomes an honest column of numbers, and every linear-algebra computation reduces to arithmetic you already know. In quantum mechanics, choosing a basis is exactly what a physicist means by choosing a representation. Here ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ is only an early label for the vector ψ\psiψ; Dirac notation is introduced formally in Lecture 7. Finite or discrete bases give ordinary coordinate columns. Position and momentum wavefunctions are the later continuous/generalized analogue, not finite columns in ordinary Hilbert bases. By the end of today you will be able to find bases, compute dimensions, pass between a vector and its coordinates, and count dimensions of sums and intersections.

02Linear independence

A spanning list may carry redundancy: some vector may already be a combination of the others. The lists with no redundancy are the important ones.

A list v1,…,vmv_1,\dots,v_mv1​,…,vm​ is linearly independent if the only scalars a1,…,ama_1,\dots,a_ma1​,…,am​ with a1v1+⋯+amvm=0a_1v_1+\cdots+a_mv_m=0a1​v1​+⋯+am​vm​=0 are a1=⋯=am=0a_1=\cdots=a_m=0a1​=⋯=am​=0. 03The fundamental counting theorem

Everything that follows—that dimension is well defined, that bases behave as coordinate systems—rests on one inequality.

theoremIndependent ≤\le≤ spanning

In a vector space VVV, the length of any linearly independent list is at most the length of any spanning list.

Compare your reasoning

Let u1,…,umu_1,\dots,u_mu1​,…,um​ be independent and w1,…,wnw_1,\dots,w_nw1​,…,wn​ span VVV; we prove m≤nm\le nm≤n. We run a process of mmm steps, each inserting one uuu and deleting one www, keeping a spanning list of length nnn throughout.

Step 1. The list w1,…,wnw_1,\dots,w_nw1​,…,wn​ spans VVV, so adjoining u1u_1u1​ in front gives the dependent list u1,w1,…,wnu_1,w_1,\dots,w_nu1​,w1​,…,wn​ (here u1u_1u1​ lies in the span of the www’s). By lemma: Linear dependence lemma some entry is in the span of the earlier ones; it is not u1u_1u1​, since independence makes u1≠0u_1\neq0u1​=0 and span⁡( )={0}\spn(\,)=\set{0}span()={0}. So it is some wkw_kwk​, which we delete. The result is a spanning list of length nnn containing u1u_1u1​.

Step kkk (2≤k≤m)(2\le k\le m)(2≤k≤m). Suppose we hold a spanning list of length nnn containing u1,…,uk−1u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}u1​,…,uk−1​. If m>nm>nm>n, then after Step nnn we hold a spanning list of length nnn consisting of u1,…,unu_1,\dots,u_nu1​,…,un​ only (all www’s gone). A useful by-product, proved by the same idea (keep choosing vectors outside the current span until theorem: Independent ≤\le≤ spanning stops you), is that every subspace of a finite-dimensional space is itself finite-dimensional.

04Bases and dimension
definitionBasis

A basis of VVV is a list that is linearly independent and spans VVV.

A basis can always be produced, in two complementary ways: by trimming a spanning list down, or by growing an independent list up.

theoremReduction to a basis

Every spanning list of VVV can be reduced to a basis of VVV by deleting some of its vectors.

Compare your reasoning

While the list is linearly dependent, lemma: Linear dependence lemma supplies a vector lying in the span of the earlier ones; delete it, which by the same lemma leaves the span equal to VVV. Each deletion shortens a finite list, so the process halts, and it can halt only when the list is independent—an independent spanning list, i.e. a basis. ◻

theoremExtension to a basis

In a finite-dimensional space, every linearly independent list extends to a basis.

Compare your reasoning

Let u1,…,umu_1,\dots,u_mu1​,…,um​ be independent and w1,…,wnw_1,\dots,w_nw1​,…,wn​ a basis. Apply theorem: Reduction to a basis to the spanning list u1,…,um,w1,…,wnu_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_nu1​,…,um​,w1​,…,wn​. No uiu_iui​ is ever deleted: the vectors preceding it are u1,…,ui−1u_1,\dots,u_{i-1}u1​,…,ui−1​, which are independent, so uiu_iui​ is not in their span and is not a candidate for removal. Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis—reduce any finite spanning list.

The span of a list is the smallest subspace containing it. A list is independent when 000 has only the trivial representation, and a basis is an independent spanning list—equivalently, a list giving every vector unique coordinates. Every linearly independent list is no longer than any spanning list (theorem: Independent ≤\le≤ spanning); hence all bases share a length, the dimension. In an nnn-dimensional space, an independent or spanning list of length nnn is automatically a basis. Fixing an ordered basis turns each vector into a coordinate column in Fn\F^nFn; in a finite/discrete model this is what a physicist calls choosing a representation. Continuous position and momentum representations instead produce wavefunctions and require the later Hilbert-space framework. Finally, dim⁡(U+W)=dim⁡U+dim⁡W−dim⁡(U∩W)\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)dim(U+W)=dimU+dimW−dim(U∩W), with equality to dim⁡U+dim⁡W\dim U+\dim WdimU+dimW exactly for direct sums.

Span—all linear combinations of a list; the smallest subspace containing it.

Linear independence—only the trivial combination yields 000.

Basis—an independent spanning list; gives every vector unique coordinates.

Dimension—the common length of all bases (independent ≤\le≤ spanning).

Coordinate vector—the column [v]B∈Fn[v]_{\mathcal B}\in\F^n[v]B​∈Fn once a finite ordered basis is fixed; continuous representations are function-valued analogues.
