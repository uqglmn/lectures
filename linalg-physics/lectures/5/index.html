↳Why preserved directions matterMotivation, scope, and the finite-dimensional setting

Lecture 4 ended with a tantalising example: the right basis turned a reflection into the diagonal matrix diag⁡(1,−1)\diag(1,-1)diag(1,−1). The diagonal entries were the factors by which the operator stretched two special directions. This lecture names those objects—eigenvectors, the directions an operator merely scales, and eigenvalues, the scaling factors—and develops the tools to find them: the characteristic polynomial, the guaranteed existence of eigenvalues over C\CC, and upper-triangular form, in which the eigenvalues sit on the diagonal.

Unless an example is explicitly labelled otherwise, VVV is a nonzero finite-dimensional vector space over F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C} and T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V). Thus the eigenvalue set studied here is the point spectrum; in this finite setting it agrees with the general spectrum defined by failure of invertibility. Lecture 14 separates point, continuous, and residual spectrum for operators on Hilbert space.

In the finite/discrete quantum model, a self-adjoint observable has eigenvalues for projective outcomes and eigenspaces for definite values. The equation H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi=E\psiH^ψ=Eψ describes a discrete energy eigenstate. General observables may also have continuous spectrum, and atomic spectral lines record transition energy differences; Lectures 10 and 14 supply those qualifications.

01Invariant subspaces and eigenvectors

Guiding question.

Which parts of a vector space keep their identity when an operator acts? Given T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V), we look first for subspaces that TTT preserves.

definitionInvariant subspace

A subspace U⊆VU\subseteq VU⊆V is invariant under TTT when every vector that starts in UUU remains in UUU after applying TTT: u∈U⟹Tu∈U.u\in U \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Tu\in U.u∈U⟹Tu∈U. Equivalently, T(U)⊆U.T(U)\subseteq U.T(U)⊆U.

Start with familiar invariant subspaces.

The following are always invariant: V,{0},null⁡T,im⁡T.V,\qquad \set0,\qquad \nul T,\qquad \rng T.V,{0},nullT,imT. The smallest nontrivial invariant subspaces are lines. For v≠0v\neq0v=0, the line span⁡(v)\spn(v)span(v) is invariant exactly when TvTvTv is a scalar multiple of vvv.

definitionEigenvalue, eigenvector, eigenspace

A nonzero vector keeps its line under TTT precisely when Tv=λv,v≠0.Tv=\lambda v,\qquad v\neq0.Tv=λv,v=0.

  • Eigenvalue. The scalar λ∈F\lambda\in\Fλ∈F is the scale factor.

  • Eigenvector. The nonzero vector vvv is a direction that TTT preserves.

  • Eigenspace. All vectors with the same scale factor form the subspace E(λ,T)=null⁡(T−λI)={v∈V:Tv=λv},E(\lambda,T)=\nul(T-\lambda I)=\set{v\in V : Tv=\lambda v},E(λ,T)=null(T−λI)={v∈V:Tv=λv}, including the zero vector.

The set of eigenvalues is the point spectrum. In our standing finite-dimensional setting it equals spec⁡(T)\spec(T)spec(T), defined by failure of invertibility. Lecture 14 explains why this equality can fail in infinite dimension.

Three equivalent tests.

The eigenspace is a kernel, so for a scalar λ\lambdaλ we may move freely along the chain λ∈spec⁡(T)  ⟺  E(λ,T)≠{0}  ⟺  T−λI is not injective  ⟺  T−λI is not invertible.\begin{aligned} \lambda\in\spec(T) &\iff E(\lambda,T)\neq\set0 \\ &\iff T-\lambda I\text{ is not injective} \\ &\iff T-\lambda I\text{ is not invertible.} \end{aligned}λ∈spec(T)​⟺E(λ,T)={0}⟺T−λI is not injective⟺T−λI is not invertible.​ The last step uses the finite-dimensional equivalence “injective iff invertible” from Lecture 3. 02Computing eigenvalues and eigenspaces

Guiding question.

How do we turn the geometric search for preserved directions into a reliable calculation? In coordinates, the link is singularity, detected by a determinant.

Calculate, then interpret

Read invariants from a quadratic

Compare your reasoning
With the notes’ convention pA(z)=det⁡(zI−A)p_A(z)=\det(zI-A)pA​(z)=det(zI−A), the polynomial is z2−(tr⁡A)z+det⁡Az^2-(\tr A)z+\det Az2−(trA)z+detA. Its roots are the eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity.
definitionCharacteristic polynomial

For A∈Mn(F)A\in M_n(\F)A∈Mn​(F) (or for T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) via any basis), the characteristic polynomial is χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A).\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A).χA​(λ)=det(λI−A). It is monic of degree nnn. Its roots are exactly the eigenvalues of AAA: χA(λ)=0⟺λ is an eigenvalue of A.\chi_A(\lambda)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }A.χA​(λ)=0⟺λ is an eigenvalue of A.

The eigenpair method.

For a concrete matrix AAA, separate the scale-factor calculation from the direction calculation.

  1. Form the characteristic polynomial. χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A).\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A).χA​(λ)=det(λI−A).

  2. Find the possible scale factors. χA(λ)=0.\chi_A(\lambda)=0.χA​(λ)=0.

  3. Find the directions for each root. E(λ,A)=ker⁡(A−λI).E(\lambda,A)=\ker(A-\lambda I).E(λ,A)=ker(A−λI).

  4. Check the complete result. Compare the eigenvalues with tr⁡A\tr AtrA and det⁡A\det AdetA, and count the independent eigenvectors.

Do not stop at the roots.

The polynomial finds possible scale factors; the kernel calculation finds the directions. Reporting roots without eigenspaces gives only half of the eigenpair calculation.

remarkDeterminant facts we useOpen the observation

This course uses four determinant facts without reproving them here.

  • Invertibility. A square matrix is invertible iff det⁡A≠0\det A\neq0detA=0.

  • Products. det⁡(AB)=det⁡A det⁡B\det(AB)=\det A\,\det Bdet(AB)=detAdetB. In particular, det⁡(P−1AP)=det⁡A.\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det A.det(P−1AP)=detA.

  • Triangular form. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.

  • Coefficients. det⁡(λI−A)=λn−(tr⁡A)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndet⁡A.\det(\lambda I-A) =\lambda^n-(\tr A)\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det A.det(λI−A)=λn−(trA)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndetA.

Each follows from cofactor expansion or row reduction and is proved in the course texts.

Why the roots are eigenvalues.

The key implications form one chain: χA(λ)=0  ⟺  λI−A is singular  ⟺  ker⁡(λI−A)≠{0}  ⟺  λ is an eigenvalue.\chi_A(\lambda)=0 \iff \lambda I-A\text{ is singular} \iff \ker(\lambda I-A)\neq\set0 \iff \lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue.}χA​(λ)=0⟺λI−A is singular⟺ker(λI−A)={0}⟺λ is an eigenvalue.

Why the polynomial belongs to the operator.

If two matrices represent the same operator in different bases, they are similar. Then det⁡(λI−P−1AP)=det⁡ ⁣(P−1(λI−A)P)=det⁡(λI−A),\det(\lambda I-P^{-1}AP)=\det\!\bigl(P^{-1}(\lambda I-A)P\bigr)=\det(\lambda I-A),det(λI−P−1AP)=det(P−1(λI−A)P)=det(λI−A), and therefore χP−1AP=χA.\chi_{P^{-1}AP}=\chi_A.χP−1AP​=χA​. Thus the characteristic polynomial and its eigenvalues do not depend on the chosen basis.

Why this matters.

Different eigenvalues automatically produce different independent directions. No row reduction of the eigenvectors is required.

corollaryAt most dim⁡V\dim VdimV eigenvalues

An operator on an nnn-dimensional space has at most nnn distinct eigenvalues.

Compare your reasoning

Upper-triangular form—exists over C\CC; the eigenvalues are then the diagonal entries.
