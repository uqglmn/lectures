Lecture 4 ended with a tantalising example: the right basis turned a reflection into the diagonal matrix diag⁡(1,−1)\diag(1,-1)diag(1,−1). The diagonal entries were the factors by which the operator stretched two special directions. This lecture names those objects—eigenvectors, the directions an operator merely scales, and eigenvalues, the scaling factors—and develops the tools to find them: the characteristic polynomial, the guaranteed existence of eigenvalues over C\CC, and upper-triangular form, in which the eigenvalues sit on the diagonal.

The stakes for physics could hardly be higher. A quantum observable is an operator; its eigenvalues are the values a measurement can return, and its eigenvectors are the states with a definite value. The Hamiltonian’s eigenvalues are the allowed energies, and the time-independent Schrödinger equation H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi=E\psiH^ψ=Eψ is nothing but the statement that ψ\psiψ is an eigenvector. Everything from spectral lines to the stability of matter is an eigenvalue problem.

Invariant subspaces and eigenvectors

Given T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V), the cleanest way to understand TTT is to break VVV into pieces that TTT preserves.

definitionInvariant subspace

A subspace U⊆VU\subseteq VU⊆V is invariant under T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) if Tu∈UTu\in UTu∈U for every u∈Uu\in Uu∈U, i.e. T(U)⊆UT(U)\subseteq UT(U)⊆U.

The whole space VVV, the zero subspace {0}\set0{0}, the kernel null⁡T\nul TnullT, and the range im⁡T\rng TimT are always invariant. The richest information, though, comes from the smallest nontrivial invariant subspaces—the one-dimensional ones. A one-dimensional subspace span⁡(v)\spn(v)span(v) (with v≠0v\neq0v=0) is invariant exactly when TvTvTv is a scalar multiple of vvv.

03

Existence of eigenvalues over C\CC

Over R\RR an operator may have no eigenvalue at all (the rotation). Over C\CC the situation is completely different, and the reason is the fundamental theorem of algebra.

Pause and locate the assumption

Where does the complex field matter?

The vectors v,Tv,…,Tnvv,Tv,\ldots,T^nvv,Tv,…,Tnv must be dependent over either R\RR or C\CC. So why does the theorem promise an eigenvalue only over C\CC?

The decisive step is factoring the resulting polynomial into linear factors. The fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees this over C\CC, but not over R\RR.
theoremEigenvalues exist over C\CC

Every operator on a finite-dimensional, nonzero, complex vector space has an eigenvalue.

proofProofReveal the argument

Let dim⁡V=n≥1\dim V=n\ge1dimV=n≥1 and pick any v≠0v\neq0v=0. The n+1n+1n+1 vectors v,Tv,T2v,…,Tnvv,Tv,T^2v,\dots,T^nvv,Tv,T2v,…,Tnv cannot be independent, so some nontrivial combination vanishes: a0v+a1Tv+⋯+anTnv=0,a_0v+a_1Tv+\cdots+a_nT^nv=0,a0​v+a1​Tv+⋯+an​Tnv=0, with not all aka_kak​ zero (and the top nonzero coefficient defining a polynomial of degree m≥1m\ge1m≥1). Substituting TTT for zzz and applying to vvv, 0=c (T−λ1I)⋯(T−λmI) v.0=c\,(T-\lambda_1I)\cdots(T-\lambda_mI)\,v .0=c(T−λ1​I)⋯(T−λm​I)v. remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

This is the deepest reason quantum mechanics is built over C\CC: it guarantees that observables have eigenvalues—possible measurement outcomes—and, with the extra structure of Lecture 9, that there are enough eigenvectors to form a basis. Over R\RR no such guarantee exists.

propositionCommuting operators share an eigenvector

If VVV is a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space and S,T∈L(V)S,T\in\Lin(V)S,T∈L(V) commute, then SSS and TTT have a common eigenvector.

proofProofReveal the argument

Let λ\lambdaλ be an eigenvalue of SSS ([thm:eigexist]). The restriction of TTT to the nonzero complex space E(λ,S)E(\lambda,S)E(λ,S) has an eigenvector vvv (theorem: Eigenvalues exist over C\CC again), and this vvv is an eigenvector of TTT by construction and of SSS because it lies in E(λ,S)E(\lambda,S)E(λ,S). ◻

This is the abstract statement behind compatible observables: commuting operators can be simultaneously diagonalized, so they admit states of simultaneously definite values, whereas non-commuting ones cannot.

04

The characteristic polynomial

Equation equation says eigenvalues are the scalars making T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI singular. In coordinates, singularity is detected by a determinant.

remarkDeterminant facts we useOpen the observation

Beyond the cofactor expansion of your prerequisites, this course freely uses four facts about determinants, recorded here for reference: (i) a square matrix is invertible iff det⁡A≠0\det A\neq0detA=0; (ii) the product rule det⁡(AB)=det⁡A det⁡B\det(AB)=\det A\,\det Bdet(AB)=detAdetB, whence det⁡(P−1AP)=det⁡A\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det Adet(P−1AP)=detA, so the determinant is a similarity invariant and belongs to the operator; (iii) the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries; (iv) expanding det⁡(λI−A)\det(\lambda I-A)det(λI−A) in powers of λ\lambdaλ gives λn−(tr⁡A)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndet⁡A\lambda^n-(\tr A)\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det Aλn−(trA)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndetA. Each follows from the cofactor expansion (or row reduction) and is proved in any of the course texts; we take them as given.

A root of χA\chi_AχA​ has an algebraic multiplicity (its multiplicity as a root) and a geometric multiplicity (dim⁡E(λ,T)\dim E(\lambda,T)dimE(λ,T)). They can differ.

propositionGeometric ≤\le≤ algebraic

For each eigenvalue, the geometric multiplicity does not exceed the algebraic multiplicity.

When some eigenvalue has geometric multiplicity strictly less than its algebraic multiplicity, the operator has too few eigenvectors to form a basis; it is called defective and cannot be diagonalized. The shear (1101)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)(10​11​) is the standard culprit: χ(λ)=(λ−1)2\chi(\lambda)=(\lambda-1)^2χ(λ)=(λ−1)2 (algebraic multiplicity 222), yet E(1,T)=span⁡((1,0))E(1,T)=\spn\bigl((1,0)\bigr)E(1,T)=span((1,0)) is only one-dimensional.

The fix is to relax what we ask of an eigenvector. The next definition, theorem, and example—generalized eigenvectors—are a bridge to the Jordan material of Lecture 6 and are offered for self-study; the rest of the course does not depend on them.

Upper-triangular matrices

Even when an operator cannot be diagonalized, over C\CC it can always be brought to upper-triangular form, which is nearly as useful: the eigenvalues are displayed on the diagonal.

propositionTriangular form encodes invariance

Let v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ be a basis of VVV. The matrix of TTT in this basis is upper triangular if and only if Tvk∈span⁡(v1,…,vk)Tv_k\in\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)Tvk​∈span(v1​,…,vk​) for each kkk—equivalently, each span⁡(v1,…,vk)\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)span(v1​,…,vk​) is invariant under TTT.

proofProofReveal the argument

The kkk-th column of the matrix expresses TvkTv_kTvk​ in the basis; it has zeros below the diagonal exactly when TvkTv_kTvk​ uses only v1,…,vkv_1,\dots,v_kv1​,…,vk​. The invariance reformulation follows because the spans are nested. ◻

The payoff is that for triangular matrices the spectrum is read off instantly.

theoremEigenvalues sit on the diagonal

If TTT has an upper-triangular matrix with diagonal entries λ1,…,λn\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_nλ1​,…,λn​ in some basis, then spec⁡(T)={λ1,…,λn}\spec(T)=\set{\lambda_1,\dots, \lambda_n}spec(T)={λ1​,…,λn​}. Moreover TTT is invertible iff no diagonal entry is 000.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

In quantum mechanics the eigenvalue equation H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi=E\psiH^ψ=Eψ is the time-independent Schrödinger equation: the eigenvalues EEE are the allowed energies and the eigenvectors ψ\psiψ are the stationary states. Because physical observables are represented by self-adjoint operators (Lecture 9), their eigenvalues turn out to be real and their eigenvectors can be chosen to form an orthonormal basis—the spectral theorem of Lecture 10. The abstract existence theorem above is what makes those measured values exist in the first place.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

The discreteness of atomic spectral lines is, mathematically, the discreteness of a Hamiltonian’s eigenvalues: bound-state energies form a separated set because the relevant operator has a discrete spectrum—a phenomenon made precise for infinite-dimensional spaces in Lectures 13–14.

An eigenvector is a direction an operator only rescales, and the scale factor is its eigenvalue; the eigenspace E(λ,T)=null⁡(T−λI)E(\lambda,T)=\nul(T-\lambda I)E(λ,T)=null(T−λI) collects them, and λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue exactly when T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI fails to be invertible. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent, so an nnn-dimensional space admits at most nnn eigenvalues, and nnn distinct ones force a diagonalizing basis. Over C\CC every operator has an eigenvalue—the fundamental theorem of algebra in disguise—while over R\RR it may have none. The characteristic polynomial χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A)\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)χA​(λ)=det(λI−A) has the eigenvalues as its roots, is a similarity invariant, and yields tr⁡A=∑λk\tr A=\sum\lambda_ktrA=∑λk​, det⁡A=∏λk\det A=\prod\lambda_kdetA=∏λk​. Finally, over C\CC every operator is upper-triangularizable, and then its eigenvalues are simply the diagonal entries.
