The central thread

An eigenvector is a direction an operator only rescales, and the scale factor is its eigenvalue; the eigenspace E(λ,T)=null⁡(T−λI)E(\lambda,T)=\nul(T-\lambda I)E(λ,T)=null(T−λI) collects them, and λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue exactly when T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI fails to be invertible. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent, so an nnn-dimensional space admits at most nnn eigenvalues, and nnn distinct ones force a diagonalizing basis. Over C\CC every operator has an eigenvalue—the fundamental theorem of algebra in disguise—while over R\RR it may have none. The characteristic polynomial χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A)\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)χA​(λ)=det(λI−A) has the eigenvalues as its roots, is a similarity invariant, and yields tr⁡A=∑λk\tr A=\sum\lambda_ktrA=∑λk​, det⁡A=∏λk\det A=\prod\lambda_kdetA=∏λk​. Finally, over C\CC every operator is upper-triangularizable, and then its eigenvalues are simply the diagonal entries.

Misconception check

“Every non-zero vector is an eigenvector for some eigenvalue.”

For a fixed operator, what special relationship must hold between vvv and TvTvTv?

Reveal the test

TvTvTv must be parallel to vvv. Most directions are turned into a different line and therefore are not eigenvectors.

Key concepts6 ideas to retain
  • 01Eigenvalue, eigenvector, eigenspace—Tv=λvTv=\lambda vTv=λv; E(λ,T)=null⁡(T−λI)E(\lambda,T)=\nul(T-\lambda I)E(λ,T)=null(T−λI); λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue   ⟺  T−λI\iff T-\lambda I⟺T−λI is not invertible.
  • 02Independence of eigenvectors—distinct eigenvalues give independent eigenvectors; at most dim⁡V\dim VdimV of them.
  • 03Existence over C\CC—every operator on a complex space has an eigenvalue (fundamental theorem of algebra).
  • 04Characteristic polynomial—χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A)\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)χA​(λ)=det(λI−A); its roots are the eigenvalues, with tr⁡A=∑λk\tr A=\sum\lambda_ktrA=∑λk​, det⁡A=∏λk\det A=\prod\lambda_kdetA=∏λk​.
  • 05Upper-triangular form—exists over C\CC; the eigenvalues are then the diagonal entries.
  • 06Generalized eigenvectors—the generalized-eigenspace decomposition behind Jordan form (self-study).

Worked example
A two-level system: the Pauli matrix σx\sigma_xσx​

The spin observable σx=(0110)\sigma_x=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)σx​=(01​10​) (in units of ℏ/2\hbar/2ℏ/2) has χ(λ)=λ2−1\chi(\lambda)=\lambda^2-1χ(λ)=λ2−1, so its eigenvalues are ±1\pm1±1—the two possible outcomes of measuring spin along xxx. The eigenvectors are 12(1,1)\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)2​1​(1,1) for +1+1+1 and 12(1,−1)\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)2​1​(1,−1) for −1-1−1, the “spin-right'' and “spin-left'' states. Measuring σx\sigma_xσx​ returns one of its eigenvalues and leaves the system in the matching eigenvector—the physical meaning of the spectrum.
