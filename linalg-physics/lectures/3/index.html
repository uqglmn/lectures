↳From input-output rules to structureMotivation, scope, and the finite-dimensional setting

We have the objects of linear algebra—vector spaces—and their coordinate systems—bases. Now we study the maps between vector spaces that respect this structure. A linear map is a function that commutes with addition and scalar multiplication; equivalently, it preserves linear combinations, so it preserves superposition.

The quantum language in the next sentence is a forward preview, not an assumed prerequisite; inner products and these operator classes are defined in Lectures 7–10 and are not used in this lecture’s proofs. For now, read “self-adjoint” as the special kind of linear operator used to encode an observable, “unitary” as a linear transformation that preserves quantum lengths and overlaps, and “antiunitary” as its conjugate-linear symmetry analogue. A spectral projector selects the part of a state associated with one possible outcome; the later Born rule turns the squared length of that part into a probability, and the conditional update replaces the state by the normalized selected part. Thus the finite model uses self-adjoint operators for observables, unitary operators for closed-system evolution, unitary or antiunitary symmetries, and a conditional update that is not the observable’s linear action.

Two subspaces measure how a linear map behaves: its kernel (what it sends to zero) and its image (what it can produce). The central result of the lecture, the rank–nullity theorem, ties their dimensions together in a single equation dim⁡V=dim⁡null⁡T+dim⁡im⁡T\dim V=\dim\nul T+\dim\rng TdimV=dimnullT+dimimT, and from it we will read off when linear systems are solvable and when operators are invertible.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

Once bases of finite-dimensional VVV and WWW are fixed, addition, scaling, and composition of linear maps become exactly addition, scaling, and multiplication of their matrices—the subject of Lecture 4. In that finite setting one can show dim⁡L(V,W)=(dim⁡V)(dim⁡W)\dim\Lin(V,W)=(\dim V)(\dim W)dimL(V,W)=(dimV)(dimW); the proof is cleanest in matrix language, so we defer it. Quotient spaces appear at the foundations of modern physics.

Gauge symmetry. In electromagnetism the 444-potential AμA_\muAμ​ is not unique: two potentials related by Aμ→Aμ+∂μχA_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\chiAμ​→Aμ​+∂μ​χ produce the same field strength Fμν=∂μAν−∂νAμF_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\muFμν​=∂μ​Aν​−∂ν​Aμ​. A linear map preserves linear combinations, hence superposition. It is fixed by its values on a basis, which may be chosen freely (theorem: Maps are determined by a basis). Its kernel null⁡T\nul TnullT and image im⁡T\rng TimT are subspaces; TTT is injective exactly when null⁡T={0}\nul T=\set0nullT={0} and surjective exactly when im⁡T=W\rng T=WimT=W.
