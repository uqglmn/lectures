Concept summary

An operator is diagonalizable iff it has a basis of eigenvectors, equivalently iff its eigenspace dimensions sum to dim⁡V\dim VdimV; having dim⁡V\dim VdimV distinct eigenvalues is a convenient sufficient condition. In an eigenbasis A=PDP−1A=PDP^{-1}A=PDP−1, so powers and functions are computed one eigenvalue at a time: Ak=PDkP−1A^k=PD^kP^{-1}Ak=PDkP−1 and f(T)=Pf(D)P−1f(T)=Pf(D)P^{-1}f(T)=Pf(D)P−1, with the exponential etAe^{tA}etA solving x˙=Ax\dot x=Axx˙=Ax. Every operator satisfies its own characteristic equation χT(T)=0\chi_T(T)=0χT​(T)=0 (Cayley–Hamilton), which expresses inverses and high powers as low-degree polynomials in TTT. Spectral mapping sends eigenvalues λ\lambdaλ to f(λ)f(\lambda)f(λ); in quantum mechanics this is exactly why diagonalizing the Hamiltonian solves the time evolution e−iH^t/ℏe^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}e−iH^t/ℏ.
