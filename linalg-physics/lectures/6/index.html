The central thread

On a finite-dimensional real or complex space, an operator is diagonalizable iff it has a basis of eigenvectors, equivalently iff its eigenspace dimensions sum to dim⁡V\dim VdimV; having dim⁡V\dim VdimV distinct eigenvalues is a convenient sufficient condition. In an eigenbasis A=PDP−1A=PDP^{-1}A=PDP−1, so powers and functions are computed one eigenvalue at a time: Ak=PDkP−1A^k=PD^kP^{-1}Ak=PDkP−1 and f(T)=Pf(D)P−1f(T)=Pf(D)P^{-1}f(T)=Pf(D)P−1 for f:spec⁡(T)→Ff:\spec(T)\to\Ff:spec(T)→F, with the finite matrix exponential etAe^{tA}etA solving x˙=Ax\dot x=Axx˙=Ax. Every such operator satisfies its own characteristic equation χT(T)=0\chi_T(T)=0χT​(T)=0 (Cayley–Hamilton), which expresses inverses and high powers as low-degree polynomials in TTT. Spectral mapping sends eigenvalues λ\lambdaλ to f(λ)f(\lambda)f(λ); in quantum mechanics this is exactly why diagonalizing the Hamiltonian solves the time evolution e−iH^t/ℏe^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}e−iH^t/ℏ.

Misconception check

“A repeated eigenvalue makes an operator non-diagonalizable.”

What matters: the repetition itself, or the number of independent eigenvectors available?

Reveal the test

Repetition is allowed. Diagonalisation fails only when the eigenspaces do not supply enough independent eigenvectors for a basis.

Close the spectral-mapping argument

Why does showing f(T)v=f(λ)vf(T)v=f(\lambda)vf(T)v=f(λ)v for every eigenvector prove that f(T)f(T)f(T) has no additional eigenvalues when TTT is diagonalizable?

Reveal the reasoning rubric

Rubric: choose an eigenbasis of TTT. The same basis diagonalizes f(T)f(T)f(T), and its diagonal entries are exactly f(λ1),…,f(λn)f(\lambda_1),\ldots,f(\lambda_n)f(λ1​),…,f(λn​). A diagonal matrix has no eigenvalues beyond its diagonal entries, so the displayed set is exhaustive.

Worked example
Normal modes of two coupled masses

Two equal masses sit between fixed supports, with three identical springs: one from each support to its adjacent mass and one between the masses. In unit-mass, unit-spring coordinates they obey x¨=−Kx\ddot x=-Kxx¨=−Kx with K=(2−1−12)K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)K=(2−1​−12​) in suitable units. The eigenvalues of KKK are 111 and 333, with eigenvectors (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) and (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1). In these normal coordinates the system decouples into two independent oscillators: the in-phase mode (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) at angular frequency ω=1\omega=1ω=1 and the out-of-phase mode (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1) at ω=3\omega=\sqrt3ω=3​. Diagonalizing KKK is finding the normal modes.
