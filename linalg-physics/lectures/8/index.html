↳Build orthogonality constructivelyMotivation, conventions, and the finite-dimensional physics setting

Lecture 7 ended with a finite-dimensional question: can we find a basis of mutually orthogonal unit vectors, and expand every vector as a finite sum in it? Over R\RR or C\CC, this lecture answers yes. The Gram–Schmidt procedure manufactures an orthonormal basis from any basis, and in such a basis everything becomes simple: coordinates are just inner products, v=∑k⟨ek,v⟩ ekv=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}\,e_kv=∑k​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​; the norm is the Pythagorean sum ∥v∥2=∑k∣⟨ek,v⟩∣2\norm v^2=\sum_k\abs{\inner{e_k}{v}}^2∥v∥2=∑k​∣⟨ek​,v⟩∣2; and the identity resolves as ∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek∣=I\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}=I∑k​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣=I. We then use orthonormality to solve the best-approximation problem (orthogonal projection finds the closest point of a subspace) and to prove the Riesz representation theorem, which says every bra is the partner of a unique ket. These are the daily tools of quantum mechanics: expanding a state in an energy eigenbasis, computing amplitudes, and projecting onto measurement outcomes.

definitionOrthonormal list and basis

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

In Dirac notation the expansion reads ∣v⟩=∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek,v⟩=(∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek∣)∣v⟩,\ket v=\sum_k\ket{e_k}\inner{e_k}{v} =\Bigl(\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}\Bigr)\ket v,∣v⟩=k∑​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​,v⟩=(k∑​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣)∣v⟩, so an orthonormal basis satisfies the completeness relation ∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek∣=I\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}=I∑k​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣=I. remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

Collecting the relations vk=∑j≤krjkejv_k=\sum_{j\le k}r_{jk}e_jvk​=∑j≤k​rjk​ej​ into matrices says A=QRA=QRA=QR: any matrix with independent columns factors as a matrix QQQ with orthonormal columns times an upper-triangular RRR. remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

For a fixed ordered independent input, the displayed normalization ek=wk/∥wk∥e_k=w_k/\norm{w_k}ek​=wk​/∥wk​∥ gives a unique ordered output. Changing the input order changes that output; deliberately changing signs or complex phases afterwards produces other ONBs. Every nonzero complex space, and every real space of dimension at least two, has infinitely many ONBs; R\RR has only (1)(1)(1) and (−1)(-1)(−1), while the zero space has one. Any two ONBs are related by a unitary change of basis—the inner-product-preserving operators of Lecture 9.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

In floating-point arithmetic the classical formula can lose orthogonality through accumulated rounding. Modified Gram–Schmidt, which subtracts each projection as soon as it is computed, is usually more stable than the classical form; Householder QR is a standard robust choice for dense least-squares work. The mathematics is identical, the numerical behaviour is not.

Here VVV is finite-dimensional over F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C}, and every basis sum is finite. ONB coordinates, Parseval, identity resolution, and Gram–Schmidt are finite formulas. For U⊆VU\subseteq VU⊆V, projection gives V=U⊕U⊥V=U\oplus U^\perpV=U⊕U⊥, best approximation, and Bessel. Finite Riesz gives each φ:V→F\varphi:V\to\Fφ:V→F a unique representative, with the field-dependent ket-to-bra behavior.
