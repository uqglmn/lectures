↳Geometry from an inner productMotivation, conventions, and the finite-dimensional physics setting

So far our vector spaces have had no geometry: no length, no angle, no notion of two vectors being perpendicular. The inner product supplies all three at once, and with it linear algebra becomes the language of quantum mechanics. The overlap ⟨ϕ|ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is a probability amplitude; the normalization ⟨ψ|ψ⟩=1\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1 fixes total probability; orthogonality ⟨ϕ|ψ⟩=0\braket{\phi}{\psi}=0⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩=0 means two pure states can be perfectly distinguished by a suitable projective measurement. This lecture sets up inner products in the physics convention and in Dirac’s bra–ket notation, and proves the inequality that underlies every amplitude bound in the subject: Cauchy–Schwarz.

A word on convention. We take the inner product to be conjugate-linear in the first slot and linear in the second—the standard physics convention, so that ⟨ϕ|ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩. Many mathematics texts put the conjugation in the second slot; every formula converts by swapping the two arguments.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

The mathematical core is finite-dimensional over R\RR or C\CC; quantum examples use complex spaces. A normalized ket is a representative of a pure-state ray, so ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ and eiθ∣ψ⟩e^{i\theta}\ket\psieiθ∣ψ⟩ represent the same physical state. The measurement examples use the finite ideal-projective model, not the most general quantum measurement.

01Inner products
definitionInner product, physics convention

An inner product on a complex vector space VVV assigns to each ordered pair u,v∈Vu,v\in Vu,v∈V a scalar ⟨u,v⟩∈C\inner{u}{v}\in\C⟨u,v⟩∈C such that

  1. conjugate symmetry: ⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩‾\inner{u}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{u}}⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩​;

  2. linearity in the second slot: ⟨u,av+bw⟩=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨u,w⟩\inner{u}{av+bw}=a\inner{u}{v}+b\inner{u}{w}⟨u,av+bw⟩=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨u,w⟩;

  3. positive definiteness: ⟨v,v⟩≥0\inner{v}{v}\ge0⟨v,v⟩≥0, with equality iff v=0v=0v=0.

A vector space with an inner product is an inner product space.

Conjugate symmetry forces ⟨v,v⟩=⟨v,v⟩‾\inner{v}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{v}}⟨v,v⟩=⟨v,v⟩​ to be real, so condition (3) makes sense. Combining (1) and (2) gives conjugate-linearity in the first slot: ⟨au+bw,v⟩=⟨v,au+bw⟩‾=a⟨v,u⟩+b⟨v,w⟩‾=a‾ ⟨u,v⟩+b‾ ⟨w,v⟩.\inner{au+bw}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{au+bw}} =\conj{a\inner{v}{u}+b\inner{v}{w}} =\conj a\,\inner{u}{v}+\conj b\,\inner{w}{v}.⟨au+bw,v⟩=⟨v,au+bw⟩​=a⟨v,u⟩+b⟨v,w⟩​=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨w,v⟩. The scalars come out of the first slot conjugated and out of the second slot unchanged—“one and a half” times linear, or sesquilinear. Over R\RR the conjugations vanish and an inner product is just a symmetric, positive-definite bilinear form.

02Dirac notation

Physicists write vectors as kets ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ and pair them using bras. To each vector ϕ\phiϕ Dirac associates the bra ⟨ϕ∣\bra{\phi}⟨ϕ∣, the linear functional that sends ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ to the inner product: ⟨ϕ∣ ⁣:∣ψ⟩⟼⟨ϕ|ψ⟩=⟨ϕ,ψ⟩.\bra{\phi}\colon\ket{\psi}\longmapsto\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\inner{\phi}{\psi}.⟨ϕ∣:∣ψ⟩⟼⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩=⟨ϕ,ψ⟩. Because the inner product is conjugate-linear in its first slot, the correspondence ∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣\ket{\phi}\mapsto\bra{\phi}∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣ is conjugate-linear:  a∣ϕ⟩+b∣χ⟩↦a‾ ⟨ϕ∣+b‾ ⟨χ∣\,a\ket{\phi}+b\ket{\chi}\mapsto\conj a\,\bra{\phi}+\conj b\,\bra{\chi}a∣ϕ⟩+b∣χ⟩↦a⟨ϕ∣+b⟨χ∣. The bracket ⟨ϕ|ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is then linear in the ket and conjugate-linear in the bra—exactly definition: Inner product, physics convention.

The reversed product ∣ψ⟩⟨ϕ∣\ketbra{\psi}{\phi}∣ψ⟩⟨ϕ∣ is an operator: it sends ∣χ⟩\ket{\chi}∣χ⟩ to ∣ψ⟩⟨ϕ|χ⟩\ket{\psi}\braket{\phi}{\chi}∣ψ⟩⟨ϕ∣χ⟩, a scalar multiple of ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩. Such rank-one “outer products” build projections and, in Lecture 8, resolutions of the identity.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

For normalized states, ⟨ϕ|ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is the probability amplitude to find a system prepared in ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ to be in ∣ϕ⟩\ket{\phi}∣ϕ⟩, and ∣⟨ϕ|ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is the probability. Cauchy–Schwarz below will show this probability never exceeds 111.

03Norm, orthogonality, and the Pythagorean theorem
definitionNorm and orthogonality

The norm of vvv is ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩\norm{v}=\sqrt{\inner{v}{v}}∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​. A vector with ∥v∥=1\norm{v}=1∥v∥=1 is a unit vector; in the quantum examples it is a normalized representative of a pure-state ray, not a distinct physical state for every global phase. Two vectors are orthogonal, u⊥vu\perp vu⊥v, if ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner{u}{v}=0⟨u,v⟩=0.

The norm is homogeneous, ∥av∥=⟨av,av⟩=a‾ a ⟨v,v⟩=∣a∣ ∥v∥\norm{av}=\sqrt{\inner{av}{av}} =\sqrt{\conj a\,a\,\inner{v}{v}}=\abs a\,\norm v∥av∥=⟨av,av⟩​=aa⟨v,v⟩​=∣a∣∥v∥, and vanishes only at 000. The single most useful identity relating norm and orthogonality is the following.

06Physics bridge: states, amplitudes, and what comes next

In the finite pure-state model, a unit vector ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ represents a ray, since an overall phase is unobservable. The amplitude ⟨ϕ|ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ governs interference, and ∣⟨ϕ|ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is the Born probability for the rank-one projective outcome ∣ϕ⟩\ket\phi∣ϕ⟩. Orthogonal pure states (⟨ϕ|ψ⟩=0\braket{\phi}{\psi}=0⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩=0) can be distinguished with certainty by a suitable projective measurement; an arbitrary measurement need not distinguish them. For a finite-dimensional self-adjoint observable, distinct scalar outcomes are eigenvalues and their spectral eigenspaces are orthogonal (Lecture 9); the outcomes themselves are numbers, not vectors.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

A complex inner product space that is also complete—every Cauchy sequence of vectors converges to a vector in the space—is a Hilbert space. Every finite-dimensional inner product space is automatically complete, so the distinction is invisible here; the infinite-dimensional state spaces of quantum mechanics, such as L2L^2L2, require completeness as a genuine extra axiom, taken up in Lecture 13.

That raises the finite-dimensional question driving the next lecture: can we choose a basis of mutually orthogonal unit vectors—an orthonormal basis—and expand any vector as a finite sum in it? The answer is yes, by the Gram–Schmidt procedure, and in such a basis the inner product becomes the familiar sum ∑kuk‾vk\sum_k\conj{u_k}v_k∑k​uk​​vk​ and operators acquire especially clean matrices. An inner product is sesquilinear (conjugate-linear in the first slot, linear in the second, in the physics convention), conjugate-symmetric, and positive definite; in Dirac notation ⟨ϕ|ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket and conjugate-linear in the bra. It induces a norm ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩\norm v=\sqrt{\inner v v}∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​, orthogonality ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner u v=0⟨u,v⟩=0, and the Pythagorean theorem. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥\abs{\inner u v}\le\norm u\norm v∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥ follows from orthogonal decomposition and bounds every amplitude; from it come the triangle inequality and, with the parallelogram law and polarization identity, the full geometry of the space. In the finite pure-state model, normalized vectors represent rays, ∣⟨ϕ|ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket\phi\psi}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is a transition probability, and a suitable projective measurement distinguishes orthogonal pure states—the setting in which the finite spectral theorem will live.
