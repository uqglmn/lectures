Vector Spaces and Subspaces

4 learning objectives5 topics27 exercises

Before the lecture

What you will learn

  • State the vector-space axioms and check (or refute) them for concrete sets—tuples, matrices, polynomials, functions.
  • Explain why the scalar field (R\RR versus C\CC) is part of the data of a space.
  • Apply the three-part subspace test to decide whether a subset is a subspace.
  • Form sums and direct sums, and use the zero-test (or U∩W={ 0 }U\cap W=\set0U∩W={0}) to decide directness.

After the lecture

Concept summary

A vector space is a set closed under addition and scalar multiplication and obeying eight axioms; what the vectors “are'' is irrelevant. Coordinate nnn-tuples, matrices, polynomials, functions, and quantum states are all examples. A subspace is a subset that is closed under both operations and contains 000—check exactly those three things. Subspaces combine through sums, and a sum is direct (decompositions are unique) precisely when the subspaces meet only at 000. Keep C\CC, not R\RR, as the default field: quantum mechanics demands it.
  • Field—scalars you can add, multiply, and divide by; here always R\RR or C\CC.
  • Vector space—a set closed under addition and scalar multiplication satisfying eight axioms—the nature of the vectors is irrelevant.
  • Linear combination—a1v1+⋯+amvma_1v_1+\cdots+a_mv_ma1​v1​+⋯+am​vm​; closure under these is the superposition principle.
  • Subspace—a subset containing 000 and closed under both operations (the three-part test).
  • Sum and direct sum—U1+⋯+UmU_1+\cdots+U_mU1​+⋯+Um​; direct (⊕\dsum⊕) when every vector decomposes uniquely, equivalently when 000 does.
