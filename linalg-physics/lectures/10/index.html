↳From normality to spectral resolutionMotivation, conventions, and the finite-dimensional physics setting

Lecture 9 showed that a normal operator has orthogonal eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues. The spectral theorem completes the story in the most useful way possible: a normal operator on a finite-dimensional complex space has a complete orthonormal basis of eigenvectors—it is diagonal in an orthonormal basis, i.e. unitarily diagonalizable. For observables (self-adjoint operators) the eigenvalues are moreover real. This single theorem is the mathematical backbone of the finite ideal-projective model of quantum mechanics. It supplies the eigenspace projectors. The Born probabilities and the conditional Lueders update are separate physical postulates; the general Hilbert-space theory and generalized measurements are deferred to Lecture 14.

01Schur’s theorem

The proof rests on triangularizing in an orthonormal basis.

theoremSchur

Every operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis.

Compare your reasoning

By Lecture 5 (where triangularizability over C\CC is sketched), there is a basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ in which TTT is upper-triangular, i.e. each subspace Uk=span⁡(v1,…,vk)U_k=\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)Uk​=span(v1​,…,vk​) is invariant, TUk⊆UkTU_k\subseteq U_kTUk​⊆Uk​. Gram–Schmidt (Lecture 8) produces an orthonormal e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ with span⁡(e1,…,ek)=Uk\spn(e_1,\dots,e_k)=U_kspan(e1​,…,ek​)=Uk​ for every kkk, so the same subspaces stay invariant; in this orthonormal basis TTT is upper-triangular. ◻

02The complex spectral theorem
theoremComplex spectral theorem

Suppose VVV is finite-dimensional over C\CC and T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V). The following are equivalent:

  1. TTT is normal;

  2. TTT has a diagonal matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis;

  3. VVV has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of TTT.

Compare your reasoning

(2)⇔\Leftrightarrow⇔(3) is immediate, as the columns of a diagonal matrix in a basis say exactly that the basis vectors are eigenvectors. (2)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(1): a diagonal matrix and its conjugate transpose commute, so T†T=TT†T^{\adj}T=TT^{\adj}T†T=TT†.

(1)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(2): by Schur, take an orthonormal basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ with M(T)=A\Mat(T)=AM(T)=A upper-triangular. From the first column, ∥Te1∥2=∣a11∣2\norm{Te_1}^2=\abs{a_{11}}^2∥Te1​∥2=∣a11​∣2, while M(T†)=A†\Mat(T^{\adj})=A^{\adj}M(T†)=A† gives, from its first column (the conjugated first row of AAA), ∥T†e1∥2=∣a11∣2+∣a12∣2+⋯+∣a1n∣2\norm{T^{\adj}e_1}^2=\abs{a_{11}}^2+\abs{a_{12}}^2+\cdots +\abs{a_{1n}}^2​T†e1​​2=∣a11​∣2+∣a12​∣2+⋯+∣a1n​∣2. Normality forces ∥Te1∥=∥T†e1∥\norm{Te_1}=\norm{T^{\adj}e_1}∥Te1​∥=​T†e1​​ (Lecture 9), so a12=⋯=a1n=0a_{12}=\cdots=a_{1n}=0a12​=⋯=a1n​=0. Hence A=a11⊕A1A=a_{11}\oplus A_1A=a11​⊕A1​. Comparing the two diagonal blocks of A†A=AA†A^{\adj}A=AA^{\adj}A†A=AA† gives A1†A1=A1A1†A_1^{\adj}A_1=A_1A_1^{\adj}A1†​A1​=A1​A1†​, so the trailing upper-triangular block A1A_1A1​ is normal. Induction on the dimension makes A1A_1A1​ diagonal; therefore AAA is diagonal. ◻

In matrix language: AAA is normal iff A=UDU†A=UDU^{\adj}A=UDU† with UUU unitary and DDD diagonal. The columns of UUU are the orthonormal eigenvectors; conjugating by the unitary UUU (a change of orthonormal basis, Lecture 9) reveals the diagonal DDD.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

Two normal operators are unitarily equivalent if and only if they have the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities, since each is unitarily equivalent to the diagonal matrix of its eigenvalues. For normal operators the spectrum (with multiplicity) is therefore a complete invariant—unlike the non-normal case, where Jordan structure also matters.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

A finite commuting family of normal operators on a finite-dimensional complex space can be simultaneously unitarily diagonalized. Diagonalize one operator. Every other operator BBB preserves each of its eigenspaces by commutation. Since their orthogonal direct sum is all of VVV, BBB is block diagonal in this decomposition, so B†B^{\adj}B† has the adjoint blocks; comparing the blocks in B†B=BB†B^{\adj}B=BB^{\adj}B†B=BB† shows that every restriction of BBB is normal. Diagonalize the restrictions. Each remaining operator preserves the resulting common eigenspaces, so the same block argument iterates through the finite family and produces one orthonormal basis of joint eigenvectors. The core measurement application below uses the self-adjoint pair case.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

Normality is not optional. The shear (1101)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)(10​11​) is not normal and has only a one-dimensional eigenspace, so no basis—let alone an orthonormal one—consists of its eigenvectors. The spectral theorem is precisely the statement that normal is the exact dividing line for unitary diagonalizability.

03Self-adjoint operators and the real case
theoremSpectral theorem for self-adjoint operators

A self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space (real or complex) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, with all eigenvalues real. Equivalently, a Hermitian matrix factors as A=UDU†A=UDU^{\adj}A=UDU† with UUU unitary and DDD real diagonal; a real symmetric matrix factors as A=ODOTA=ODO^{\T}A=ODOT with OOO orthogonal.

Compare your reasoning

A self-adjoint operator is normal, so over C\CC the complex spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis, and the eigenvalues are real by Lecture 9.

Over R\RR the argument needs one extra step, because theorem: Schur as stated covers complex spaces. Let AAA be the real symmetric matrix of TTT in an orthonormal basis and regard it for a moment as a complex Hermitian matrix. Its characteristic polynomial is the same either way, and by Lecture 9 all of its roots are real; hence χT\chi_TχT​ splits over R\RR. The proof of theorem: Schur uses nothing about C\CC beyond the fact that χT\chi_TχT​ splits—it triangularizes and then runs Gram–Schmidt—so it applies verbatim over R\RR and yields a real orthonormal basis in which TTT is upper-triangular. A real symmetric upper-triangular matrix equals its transpose, hence is diagonal: an orthogonal eigenbasis with real entries. ◻

For the finite-dimensional ideal-projective model this is the crucial statement: every self-adjoint observable has a complete orthonormal set of eigenstates with real eigenvalues. General observables with continuous spectrum need the projection-valued measures of Lecture 14. The real version is the “principal axes theorem”—a real symmetric matrix (inertia tensor, stress tensor, quadratic form) is diagonal in a suitable set of mutually perpendicular axes.

04Spectral decomposition

Group the eigenvalues into the distinct values λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​, and let PkP_kPk​ be the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace E(λk,T)E(\lambda_k,T)E(λk​,T).

theoremSpectral decomposition

A normal operator TTT on a finite-dimensional complex space (or a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional real space) with distinct eigenvalues λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​ satisfies PjPk=δjkPk,P1+⋯+Pm=I,T=λ1P1+⋯+λmPm,P_jP_k=\delta_{jk}P_k,\qquad P_1+\cdots+P_m=I,\qquad T=\lambda_1P_1+\cdots+\lambda_mP_m,Pj​Pk​=δjk​Pk​,P1​+⋯+Pm​=I,T=λ1​P1​+⋯+λm​Pm​, with each PkP_kPk​ self-adjoint (Pk†=PkP_k^{\adj}=P_kPk†​=Pk​). The eigenspaces are mutually orthogonal (Lecture 9) and, by the spectral theorem, span VVV, so V=E(λ1)⊕⋯⊕E(λm)V=E(\lambda_1)\dsum\cdots\dsum E(\lambda_m)V=E(λ1​)⊕⋯⊕E(λm​) orthogonally. Applying both sides of the last equation to an eigenvector shows T=∑kλkPkT=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kT=∑k​λk​Pk​, and orthogonal projections are self-adjoint. Applying fff eigenvalue by eigenvalue gives f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_k f(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​. ◻

05Measurement and what comes next

The finite spectral theorem supplies the projectors used by the ideal projective measurement postulate. For a normalized pure-state representative, the separate Born postulate says that an observable A=∑kλkPkA=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kA=∑k​λk​Pk​ yields outcome λk\lambda_kλk​ with probability ∥Pk∣ψ⟩∥2\norm{P_k\ket\psi}^2∥Pk​∣ψ⟩∥2 (the Born rule), after which the state collapses to the normalized projection Pk∣ψ⟩/∥Pk∣ψ⟩∥P_k\ket\psi/\norm{P_k\ket\psi}Pk​∣ψ⟩/∥Pk​∣ψ⟩∥ when that probability is nonzero (the Lueders update postulate). remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

In the finite ideal-projective model, immediately repeating the same measurement returns the same outcome with certainty: the first Lueders update leaves the state in an eigenspace of AAA, on which AAA acts as a single eigenvalue. Commuting finite-dimensional self-adjoint observables can be diagonalized simultaneously in one orthonormal basis, so a maximal set of commuting observables (a “complete set of commuting observables”) labels states by joint eigenvalues—the quantum numbers of a system. Non-commuting observables share no eigenbasis, and the uncertainty relation of Lecture 9 measures how badly. The next lectures leave finite dimensions behind: dual spaces and tensors (Lectures 11–12), then Hilbert spaces and the spectral theorem for operators with continuous spectra (Lectures 13–14), where position and momentum finally find their home.

The finite sum A=∑kλkPkA=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kA=∑k​λk​Pk​ then becomes an integral ∫λ dP(λ)\int\lambda\,dP(\lambda)∫λdP(λ) against a projection-valued measure—the spectral theorem of Lecture 14, for which the finite-dimensional version proved here is the template.

The finite spectral theorem says a normal operator on a finite-dimensional complex space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors—equivalently a diagonal matrix in an orthonormal basis, T=UDU†T=UDU^{\adj}T=UDU† with UUU unitary. Self-adjoint operators are the special case with real eigenvalues (and a real orthogonal eigenbasis when the space is real), the principal-axes theorem. Grouping eigenspaces gives the spectral decomposition T=∑kλkPkT=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kT=∑k​λk​Pk​ with orthogonal projections satisfying PjPk=δjkPkP_jP_k=\delta_{jk}P_kPj​Pk​=δjk​Pk​ and ∑kPk=I\sum_kP_k=I∑k​Pk​=I, whence f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_kf(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​. In the finite quantum model this supplies real spectral values and eigenspace projectors. The separate Born and Lueders postulates give ideal-projective probabilities and conditional updates, and commuting observables diagonalize together.
