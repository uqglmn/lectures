The central thread

On a complex H\HilH, bounded operators have adjoints and point, continuous, and residual spectrum. Domains govern unbounded position and momentum; their exact CCR has no finite-matrix model. Compact self-adjoint operators have a kernel-completed eigenbasis and nonzero spectrum accumulating only at zero. General self-adjoint operators use domain-aware PVMs, and strongly continuous dynamics use Stone generators. The pure/projective/closed QM model sits inside density-operator, POVM/instrument, and channel formulations.

Learn the argument

Connect infinite-dimensional spectra to quantum dynamics

In infinite dimensions, spectral values need not be eigenvalues, domains matter, and compactness recovers a discrete eigenbasis in an important special case.

remark
Residual spectrum and normal operators

For completeness, the spectrum splits into three parts. If T−λIT-\lambda\idmapT−λI has a kernel, λ\lambdaλ is in the point spectrum; if it is injective with dense range but no bounded inverse, λ\lambdaλ is in the continuous spectrum; and if it is injective with range that is not even dense, λ\lambdaλ is in the residual spectrum. The right shift exhibits the third kind: λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 is residual, since SSS is injective but its range misses e1e_1e1​ by a whole dimension. For normal operators—in particular every self-adjoint observable and every unitary—the residual spectrum is empty: if the range of T−λIT-\lambda\idmapT−λI were not dense, its orthogonal complement null⁡(T†−λ‾I)\nul(T^{\adj}-\conj\lambda\idmap)null(T†−λI) would be nonzero, and for a normal operator that forces null⁡(T−λI)≠{0}\nul(T-\lambda\idmap)\neq\set0null(T−λI)={0} (Lecture 9), putting λ\lambdaλ in the point spectrum instead. This is why the observables of quantum mechanics carry only point and continuous spectrum.

remark
Domains matter

For unbounded operators, ⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{Tu}{v}=\inner u{Tv}⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩ on a domain makes TTT symmetric, but self-adjoint additionally requires the adjoint to share that domain. Bounded normal operators also have a spectral theorem, but the real-observable PVM theorem and Stone-generator role used here require a genuinely self-adjoint realization; mere symmetry on a smaller domain is insufficient. Thus fixing the domain (and the boundary conditions) is part of defining an observable.

theorem
Spectral theorem, compact self-adjoint case — cited

A compact self-adjoint operator TTT has a finite or countable set of nonzero real eigenvalues; each has finite multiplicity, and zero is their only possible accumulation point. Moreover H=null⁡T⊕im⁡T‾,\Hil=\nul T\dsum\overline{\rng T},H=nullT⊕imT, the nonzero eigenspaces span im⁡T‾\overline{\rng T}imT, and any orthonormal basis of null⁡T\nul TnullT completes them to an eigenbasis of H\HilH. In a separable infinite-dimensional space this basis may be listed as (en)(e_n)(en​) with eigenvalues λn→0\lambda_n\to0λn​→0 (including zero entries from the kernel), giving T=∑nλn∣en⟩⟨en∣T=\sum_n\lambda_n\ketbra{e_n}{e_n}T=∑n​λn​∣en​⟩⟨en​∣. In finite dimension the corresponding list and sum are finite; no limiting assertion is needed.

Spectral theorem, general self-adjoint case — cited

remark
The layered quantum dictionary

The core model uses a normalized vector as a representative of a pure-state ray, a self-adjoint observable, an ideal PVM/Lueders measurement instrument, and a strongly continuous unitary group for closed-system dynamics. The general replacements are density operators for states, POVMs together with instruments for measurements, and quantum channels for open dynamics. Finite-level systems are genuine quantum mechanics; infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is needed for continuous canonical degrees of freedom and scattering.

Misconception check

“The spectrum is just the set of eigenvalues.”

In infinite dimensions, how else can T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI fail to have a bounded inverse?

Reveal the test

After the point case, an injective failure is continuous only when the range is dense; a non-dense range is residual. For bounded normal operators the adjoint-kernel identity rules out the residual case.

A computed spectrum

Key concepts6 ideas to retain
  • 01Bounded operator—finite operator norm === continuity; adjoint via Riesz; the self-adjoint, unitary, and normal classes persist.
  • 02Spectrum—failure of bounded invertibility in B(H)\Bounded(\Hil)B(H); point, continuous (dense range), and residual (non-dense range), with no residual spectrum for normal operators.
  • 03Unbounded observables—x^\hat xx^ and p^=−iℏ d/dx\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dxp^​=−iℏd/dx obey [x^,p^]=iℏI[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\idmap[x^,p^​]=iℏI, impossible in finite dimensions (a trace argument).
  • 04Compact self-adjoint—nonzero eigenvalues have finite multiplicity and only accumulate at zero; kernel plus nonzero eigenspaces gives an eigenbasis.
  • 05General spectral theorem—A=∫λ dP(λ)A=\int\lambda\,dP(\lambda)A=∫λdP(λ) on its natural domain; pure-point PVMs reduce this to eigenprojection sums, while non-atomic components do not; strongly continuous unitary groups have self-adjoint Stone generators.
  • 06QM dictionary—pure/PVM/closed is the core model; density operators, POVMs with instruments, and channels are the general replacements.

Writing A=∫λ dP(λ)A=\int\lambda\,dP(\lambda)A=∫λdP(λ), the projection-valued measure PPP assigns to each spectral region Ω\OmegaΩ the projection onto the matching states.
