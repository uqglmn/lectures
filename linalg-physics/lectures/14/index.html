↳Beyond eigenvalues: the operator spectrumScope, domains, and the infinite-dimensional operator setting

This final lecture carries the operator theory of Part I—adjoints, the spectral theorem, the operator classes of Lecture 9—into the infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces of Lecture 13, where it becomes the mathematical language of quantum mechanics. Two things change in infinite dimensions. First, eigenvalues no longer tell the whole story: an operator can be “non-invertible in the limit” without having any eigenvector, giving a continuous spectrum. Second, the operators of physics—position, momentum, energy—are unbounded, defined only on dense domains, and treating them rigorously is essential. We build operator norms and adjoints, classify the spectrum, meet position and momentum and their canonical commutation relation, and state the spectral theorem in the form quantum mechanics needs. The course closes with the dictionary between this linear algebra and the postulates of quantum theory.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

Throughout, H\HilH is a complex Hilbert space and B(H)\Bounded(\Hil)B(H) denotes its bounded operators. The algebraic notation L(V,W)\Lin(V,W)L(V,W) retains its earlier meaning. An unbounded operator is a linear expression together with a specified dense domain; adjoints, products, commutators, and functions of such operators retain domain conditions.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

The assessable core is B(H)\Bounded(\Hil)B(H), adjoints, the three spectral parts, one shift, direct unboundedness, common-domain CCR and trace obstruction, boundary conditions, the qualified compact theorem, a domain-aware PVM theorem, and the pure/projective/closed quantum dictionary. Resolvents, compact-resolvent physics, Stone’s theorem, multiplication/free-particle models, and finite ideal measurement calculations are bridges. Repeated ladder algebra, kernel proofs, squeezed/coherent vistas, and general measurement/open-system developments are extensions.

02The spectrum
definitionSpectrum

For T∈B(H)T\in\Bounded(\Hil)T∈B(H), the spectrum is spec⁡(T)={λ∈C:T−λI has no bounded everywhere inverse in B(H)}.\spec(T)=\set{\lambda\in\C : T-\lambda\idmap \text{ has no bounded everywhere inverse in }\Bounded(\Hil)}.spec(T)={λ∈C:T−λI has no bounded everywhere inverse in B(H)}. Writing A=T−λIA=T-\lambda\idmapA=T−λI, the disjoint cases are: point spectrum when null⁡A≠{0}\nul A\ne\set0nullA={0}; continuous spectrum when AAA is injective with dense range but no bounded everywhere inverse; and residual spectrum when AAA is injective with non-dense range.

In finite dimensions “not invertible” means “has a kernel,” so the spectrum is exactly the eigenvalues. In infinite dimensions an injective operator may have dense nonclosed range (continuous spectrum) or non-dense range (residual spectrum). For a self-adjoint operator the spectrum is real; for a unitary operator it lies on the unit circle—the infinite-dimensional shadows of Lecture 9.

The complement of the spectrum is the resolvent set, where (T−λI)−1(T-\lambda\idmap)^{-1}(T−λI)−1 exists as a bounded operator. The resolvent is the analytic engine behind the spectral theorem. Optional Lecture 15 uses it to control how spectra and eigenstates respond to a perturbation.

remarkResidual spectrum and normal operatorsOpen the observation

For completeness, the spectrum splits into three parts. If T−λIT-\lambda\idmapT−λI has a kernel, λ\lambdaλ is in the point spectrum; if it is injective with dense range but no bounded inverse, λ\lambdaλ is in the continuous spectrum; and if it is injective with range that is not even dense, λ\lambdaλ is in the residual spectrum. The right shift exhibits the third kind: λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 is residual, since SSS is injective but its range misses e1e_1e1​ by a whole dimension. For normal operators—in particular every self-adjoint observable and every unitary—the residual spectrum is empty: if the range of T−λIT-\lambda\idmapT−λI were not dense, its orthogonal complement null⁡(T†−λ‾I)\nul(T^{\adj}-\conj\lambda\idmap)null(T†−λI) would be nonzero, and for a normal operator that forces null⁡(T−λI)≠{0}\nul(T-\lambda\idmap)\neq\set0null(T−λI)={0} (Lecture 9), putting λ\lambdaλ in the point spectrum instead. This is why the observables of quantum mechanics carry only point and continuous spectrum.

remarkDomains matterOpen the observation

04Compact operators and the discrete spectral theorem

The closest infinite-dimensional analogue of a Hermitian matrix is a compact self-adjoint operator.

definitionCompact operator

An operator T∈B(H)T\in\Bounded(\Hil)T∈B(H) is compact if it is a norm-limit of finite-rank operators: there are operators TNT_NTN​ with finite-dimensional range and ∥T−TN∥→0\norm{T-T_N}\to0∥T−TN​∥→0. Equivalently, TTT maps the unit ball to a set in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence—TTT repairs, on its image, exactly the compactness failure of Lecture 13.

05The spectral theorem and the postulates of quantum mechanics

For a general self-adjoint operator—bounded or unbounded—the universal form is a PVM integral. Whether it reduces to an eigenprojection sum is determined by the atomic or non-atomic character of the spectral measure, not merely by which points lie in the operator-theoretic continuous spectrum.

remarkThe layered quantum dictionaryOpen the observation

The core model uses a normalized vector as a representative of a pure-state ray, a self-adjoint observable, an ideal PVM/Lueders measurement instrument, and a strongly continuous unitary group for closed-system dynamics. The general replacements are density operators for states, POVMs together with instruments for measurements, and quantum channels for open dynamics. Finite-level systems are genuine quantum mechanics; infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is needed for continuous canonical degrees of freedom and scattering.

remarkWhy this mattersOpen the observation

The trace obstruction of proposition: No finite-dimensional model is no technicality: the canonical commutation relation, the continuous spectra of position and momentum, and the scattering states of the free particle all require an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. This does not exclude finite-level quantum systems such as spin and qubits; it identifies the extra scope required by continuous canonical degrees of freedom.

On a complex H\HilH, bounded operators have adjoints and point, continuous, and residual spectrum. Domains govern unbounded position and momentum; their exact CCR has no finite-matrix model. Compact self-adjoint operators have a kernel-completed eigenbasis and nonzero spectrum accumulating only at zero. General self-adjoint operators use domain-aware PVMs, and strongly continuous dynamics use Stone generators. The pure/projective/closed QM model sits inside density-operator, POVM/instrument, and channel formulations.
