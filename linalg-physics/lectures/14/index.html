The central thread

A bounded operator has finite operator norm and an adjoint defined through the Riesz theorem, and the self-adjoint, unitary, and normal classes of Lecture 9 persist unchanged. The spectrum spec⁡(T)={λ:T−λI not invertible}\spec(T)=\set{\lambda:T-\lambda\idmap\text{ not invertible}}spec(T)={λ:T−λI not invertible} generalizes the eigenvalues: in infinite dimensions it splits into a point spectrum (true eigenvalues) and a continuous spectrum (no eigenvector), with self-adjoint spectra real and unitary spectra on the unit circle. The observables of physics—position x^\hat xx^ and momentum p^=−iℏ d/dx\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dxp^​=−iℏd/dx—are unbounded and obey [x^,p^]=iℏI[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\idmap[x^,p^​]=iℏI, which a trace argument shows is impossible in finite dimensions. Compact self-adjoint operators diagonalize exactly as Hermitian matrices do, T=∑nλn∣en⟩⟨en∣T=\sum_n\lambda_n\ketbra{e_n}{e_n}T=∑n​λn​∣en​⟩⟨en​∣; the general spectral theorem replaces the sum by an integral ∫λ dP(λ)\int\lambda\,dP(\lambda)∫λdP(λ) against a projection-valued measure, and Stone's theorem turns a self-adjoint Hamiltonian into unitary time evolution e−iHt/ℏe^{-iHt/\hbar}e−iHt/ℏ. This is the mathematics of quantum mechanics, and the end of our fourteen lectures.

Connect infinite-dimensional spectra to quantum dynamics

In infinite dimensions, spectral values need not be eigenvalues, domains matter, and compactness recovers a discrete eigenbasis in an important special case.

definition
Bounded operator; operator norm

A linear operator T ⁣:H→HT\colon\Hil\to\HilT:H→H is bounded if ∥T∥=sup⁡v≠0∥Tv∥∥v∥<∞.\norm T=\sup_{v\neq0}\frac{\norm{Tv}}{\norm v}<\infty .∥T∥=supv=0​∥v∥∥Tv∥​<∞. This is the operator norm of Lecture 10; what is new is that in infinite dimensions the supremum can be infinite, so boundedness is a genuine hypothesis. Bounded is equivalent to continuous, and the bounded operators form a Banach space L(H)\Lin(\Hil)L(H) under the operator norm.

definition
Compact operator

An operator T∈L(H)T\in\Lin(\Hil)T∈L(H) is compact if it is a norm-limit of finite-rank operators: there are operators TNT_NTN​ with finite-dimensional range and ∥T−TN∥→0\norm{T-T_N}\to0∥T−TN​∥→0. Equivalently, TTT maps the unit ball to a set in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence—TTT repairs, on its image, exactly the compactness failure of Lecture 13.

Misconception check

“The spectrum is just the set of eigenvalues.”

In infinite dimensions, how else can T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI fail to have a bounded inverse?

Reveal the test

It may be injective but fail surjectivity, have non-closed range, or possess an unbounded inverse. Continuous spectrum has no finite-dimensional analogue.
