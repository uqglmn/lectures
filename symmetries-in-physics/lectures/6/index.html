03Discarding rigid motions

04The central theorem: symmetry block-diagonalises DDD

That GGG is a symmetry of the molecule — it permutes identical nuclei and preserves all interatomic geometry — means precisely that the potential is invariant: V(ρ(g)u)=V(u)V\bigl(\rho(g)u\bigr)=V(u)V(ρ(g)u)=V(u) for all g∈Gg\in Gg∈G and all displacements. For the quadratic form V=12 qTDqV=\frac12\,q^{\mathsf T}DqV=21​qTDq this reads ρ(g)TD ρ(g)=D\rho(g)^{\mathsf T}D\,\rho(g)=Dρ(g)TDρ(g)=D, and since the matrices ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) are orthogonal, [D, ρ(g)]=0for all g∈G.\bigl[D,\,\rho(g)\bigr]=0 \qquad\text{for all }g\in G .[D,ρ(g)]=0for all g∈G. remarkReality constraints and complex irreduciblesOpen the observation

The theorem is complex-linear, but the molecule is real. Let CCC denote complex conjugation on the complexified displacement space. Since CD=DCCD=DCCD=DC and Cρ(g)=ρ(g)CC\rho(g)=\rho(g)CCρ(g)=ρ(g)C, conjugation carries the isotypic component of V(i)V^{(i)}V(i) to that of its conjugate V(i)‾\overline{V^{(i)}}V(i). In conjugate adapted bases the reduced blocks obey F(iˉ)=F(i)‾F^{(\bar i)}=\overline{F^{(i)}}F(iˉ)=F(i), so non-real conjugate irreducibles have the same spectrum and combine into real multiplets. A complex one-dimensional sector therefore need not give a nondegenerate physical mode.

In the brief real-type language: a self-conjugate complex irrep of real type has a real form; a non-self-conjugate irrep is of complex type and must travel with its conjugate; a self-conjugate irrep of quaternionic type has no real form and occurs with the corresponding even-multiplicity constraint when a real representation is complexified. We keep the complex character-table calculus, adding only these reality constraints.

The planar rotation representation of C3C_3C3​ is the diagnostic example. On R2\R^2R2, let ccc act by R(2π/3)R(2\pi/3)R(2π/3) and J=(0−110)J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)J=(01​−10​). After complexification the representation is the sum of the conjugate one-dimensional characters χ1⊕χ2\chi_1\oplus\chi_2χ1​⊕χ2​. A real matrix commuting with the rotation has the form a1+bJa\mathbb 1+bJa1+bJ; if it is also symmetric, as a dynamical matrix is, then b=0b=0b=0. Thus D=a1D=a\mathbb 1D=a1: the two conjugate complex singlets form a reality-enforced real doublet, not an accidental coincidence.

Three comments. First, the division of labour: group theory fixes the block structure — the sizes mim_imi​, the forced multiplet factors nin_ini​, the symmetry-adapted bases — while the dynamics (spring constants, masses) only fills in the numerical entries of the small reduced matrices F(i)F^{(i)}F(i). A 3N3N3N-dimensional eigenvalue problem collapses into independent mi×mim_i\times m_imi​×mi​ problems. Second, a degeneracy larger than nin_ini​ can come from a repeated eigenvalue inside one F(i)F^{(i)}F(i), from an accidental coincidence between unrelated blocks, or from the reality pairing of Remark Reality constraints and complex irreducibles; only the last is an additional physical constraint. Lecture 7 takes this theme into quantum mechanics. Third, the mechanism of the proof — an equivariant operator forced by Schur’s lemma to be scalar across partner indices, leaving one reduced matrix element per pair of copies — is worth committing to memory: it is the Wigner–Eckart theorem in embryo.

The practical recipe, then:

  1. identify the point group GGG of the equilibrium configuration;

  2. compute χdisp(g)=Ng χvec(g)\chi_{\mathrm{disp}}(g)=N_g\,\chi_{\mathrm{vec}}(g)χdisp​(g)=Ng​χvec​(g) class by class;

  3. subtract χtrans\chi_{\mathrm{trans}}χtrans​ and χrot\chi_{\mathrm{rot}}χrot​;

  4. extract multiplicities mi=⟨χi,χvib⟩Gm_i=\inner{\chi_i}{\chi_{\mathrm{vib}}}_Gmi​=⟨χi​,χvib​⟩G​ from the character table;

  5. diagonalise each reduced F(i)F^{(i)}F(i): a reduced eigenvalue of multiplicity qqq gives complex degeneracy qniq n_iqni​, then impose the conjugate-pair reality constraints;

  6. construct explicit modes with the projectors of Lecture 5.

07Infrared and Raman activity: a preview

Which vibrations does a spectrometer actually see? Light couples to the molecular dipole moment, whose components transform like the coordinate functions (x,y,z)(x,y,z)(x,y,z); light scattering (Raman) couples to the polarisability, whose components transform like the quadratics (x2,y2,z2,xy,yz,zx)(x^2,y^2,z^2,xy,yz,zx)(x2,y2,z2,xy,yz,zx). The full justification is the selection-rule machinery of Lecture 7, but the rule itself can be stated now:

A vibrational mode is infrared active iff its irreducible representation occurs among those of (x,y,z)(x,y,z)(x,y,z), and Raman active iff it occurs among those of the quadratics.

For water, the table in Section 1.5 shows (x,y,z)(x,y,z)(x,y,z) spanning B2⊕B1⊕A1B_2\oplus B_1\oplus A_1B2​⊕B1​⊕A1​; all three vibrations (2A1⊕B12A_1\oplus B_12A1​⊕B1​) match and are infrared active — as observed, water absorbs at all three fundamentals (the reason it is a greenhouse gas). For ammonia, (x,y,z)(x,y,z)(x,y,z) spans A1⊕EA_1\oplus EA1​⊕E, so all four fundamentals 2A1⊕2E2A_1\oplus2E2A1​⊕2E are infrared active — also as observed. The rule has teeth in molecules with more symmetry: in centrosymmetric molecules (point group containing the inversion iii) the coordinates are odd under iii while the quadratics are even, so no mode is both infrared and Raman active — the mutual exclusion rule, which Lecture 7 will derive in two lines from parity and which Sheet 6 explores.
