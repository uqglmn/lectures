01Representations: definition and first examples

Lectures 1 and 2 studied groups in the abstract. Physics, however, meets a group through its action on the states of a system, and the state spaces of physics — quantum Hilbert spaces, spaces of displacements of atoms, spaces of fields — are vector spaces. The operations of the group must respect that linear structure: a symmetry operation applied to a superposition is the superposition of the transformed states. The mathematical object capturing this is the following.

definitionRepresentation

A (linear) representation of a group GGG on a finite-dimensional complex vector space VVV is a homomorphism ρ ⁣:G⟶GL(V),\rho\colon G\longrightarrow \GL(V),ρ:G⟶GL(V), where GL(V)\GL(V)GL(V) is the group of invertible linear operators on VVV. Thus to each g∈Gg\in Gg∈G is assigned an invertible operator ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g), with ρ(gh)=ρ(g) ρ(h)for all g,h∈G,\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\,\rho(h) \quad\text{for all }g,h\in G,ρ(gh)=ρ(g)ρ(h)for all g,h∈G, whence automatically ρ(e)=id⁡V\rho(e)=\id_Vρ(e)=idV​ and ρ(g−1)=ρ(g)−1\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{-1}ρ(g−1)=ρ(g)−1. The dimension (or degree) of the representation is dim⁡V\dim VdimV. We speak of “the representation (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V)”, or simply of VVV when ρ\rhoρ is understood.

Unless stated otherwise our vector spaces are complex and finite-dimensional; the real case will be flagged when it matters (and it will matter — see Example A representation that splits over C\CC but not over R\RR).

remarkScope for this part of the courseOpen the observation

This standing definition is the first rung of the representation-scope ladder in the student syllabus: algebraic finite-dimensional representations. Later in this lecture we also construct representations on function spaces, which may be infinite-dimensional, but Maschke’s theorem, finite character sums, trace identities, and dimension counts are used only under their stated finite-group and finite-dimensional hypotheses. Part II separately adds continuity, unitarity, projective actions, and domains of unbounded derived generators; those are not hidden in Definition Representation.

Notation: operators versus matrices. Throughout these notes we keep two parallel notations, and the distinction is deliberate. The symbol ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) always denotes the abstract linear operator on VVV, defined without reference to any basis. Once a basis (e1,…,en)(e_1,\dots,e_n)(e1​,…,en​) of VVV is chosen, the corresponding matrices are denoted D(g)D(g)D(g) and defined by ρ(g) ej=∑i=1nDij(g) ei,\rho(g)\,e_j=\sum_{i=1}^{n} D_{ij}(g)\,e_i ,ρ(g)ej​=i=1∑n​Dij​(g)ei​, so that the jjj-th column of D(g)D(g)D(g) lists the components of the image of the jjj-th basis vector. The homomorphism property becomes matrix multiplication, D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h), and a map D ⁣:G→GL(n,C)D\colon G\to\GL(n,\C)D:G→GL(n,C) with this property is called a matrix representation. Irreducible representations, once we have them, will carry labels ρ(i)\rho^{(i)}ρ(i), D(i)D^{(i)}D(i). Keeping operators and matrices typographically apart costs little and prevents a great deal of confusion when several bases are in play at once — as they constantly are from Section 1.6 onwards.

02How symmetries act on functions

We now come to the single most important construction of the course. The states of physical systems are very often functions on the space the symmetry group transforms: wavefunctions on configuration space, displacement fields on a molecule, temperature distributions on a body. The group’s action on points then induces an action on the functions — and it does so by a formula whose one subtle feature, an inverse, repays careful attention.

theoremThe function-space representation

Let the group GGG act on a set XXX, and let F(X)\mathcal F(X)F(X) denote the vector space of complex-valued functions on XXX. For g∈Gg\in Gg∈G and ψ∈F(X)\psi\in\mathcal F(X)ψ∈F(X) define (ρ(g)ψ)(x):=ψ(g−1⋅x).\bigl(\rho(g)\psi\bigr)(x) := \psi\bigl(g^{-1}\cdot x\bigr).(ρ(g)ψ)(x):=ψ(g−1⋅x). Then each ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is an invertible linear operator and ρ ⁣:G→GL(F(X))\rho\colon G\to\GL(\mathcal F(X))ρ:G→GL(F(X)) is a representation. The same formula defines a representation on any subspace of F(X)\mathcal F(X)F(X) stable under all the ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) (for infinite XXX one works with such subspaces — polynomials, smooth functions, square-integrable functions — as the context requires).

Why the inverse? First the conceptual answer: ρ(g)ψ\rho(g)\psiρ(g)ψ is the function ψ\psiψ carried along by ggg. Evaluating the definition at a transformed point, (ρ(g)ψ)(g⋅x)=ψ(x):\bigl(\rho(g)\psi\bigr)(g\cdot x)=\psi(x):(ρ(g)ψ)(g⋅x)=ψ(x): the transported function takes, at the transported point, the value the original function took at the original point — rotate a temperature distribution by ggg and the hot spot moves with the rotation. The function-space construction is fully general, and we shall lean on it repeatedly. One formula, the whole course.

03Equivalence of representations

When are two representations “the same”? As with groups themselves (Lecture 1), the answer is: when a structure-preserving dictionary translates one into the other.

definitionEquivalence

Representations (ρV,V)(\rho_V,V)(ρV​,V) and (ρW,W)(\rho_W,W)(ρW​,W) of the same group GGG are equivalent, written ρV≅ρW\rho_V\cong\rho_WρV​≅ρW​, if there is an invertible linear map T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W with T ρV(g)=ρW(g) Tfor all g∈G.T\,\rho_V(g)=\rho_W(g)\,T \qquad\text{for all }g\in G .TρV​(g)=ρW​(g)Tfor all g∈G. Such a TTT is called an intertwiner (it lets the group action pass through it).

In matrix language: choosing bases and letting SSS be the matrix of TTT, equivalence reads DW(g)=S DV(g) S−1D_W(g)=S\,D_V(g)\,S^{-1}DW​(g)=SDV​(g)S−1 for all ggg, with one and the same SSS. In particular the matrices of a single representation in two different bases are related this way — equivalent representations are one representation written in different coordinates, and all notions we build (irreducibility, characters, multiplicities) will be invariant under equivalence.

Example A representation that splits over C\CC but not over R\RR displayed a representation containing smaller ones. The general notions:

In the example, the invariant subspace came with an invariant complement. That this is automatic for finite groups is the content of Maschke’s theorem — and it is genuinely a theorem, not an algebraic triviality:

05Unitarity: the averaging trick

Equip VVV with a Hermitian inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}⟨⋅,⋅⟩, conjugate-linear in the first argument (the physics convention). An operator UUU is unitary if ⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩\inner{Uu}{Uv}=\inner{u}{v}⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩ for all u,vu,vu,v, and a representation is unitary (with respect to the given product) if every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is.

remarkThe averaging trickOpen the observation

The proof’s engine — average a quantity over the group, and the average is automatically invariant because translating the summation variable merely reshuffles the sum — is the most frequently reused idea in this subject. It will prove Maschke’s theorem in a moment, the orthogonality relations in Lecture 4, and the projection formulae in Lecture 5; and in Part II, with the sum replaced by an invariant integral over a compact Lie group, the entire theory will be rebuilt on it.

In matrix terms, the theorem says every matrix representation of a finite group is equivalent to one by unitary matrices: take any basis of VVV orthonormal for the invariant product; the change of basis SSS conjugates the D(g)D(g)D(g) into unitary form.

06Maschke’s theorem: complete reducibility
theoremMaschke

Every finite-dimensional complex representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations: there are invariant subspaces W1,…,WkW_1,\dots,W_kW1​,…,Wk​, each irreducible, with V=W1⊕W2⊕⋯⊕Wk.V=W_1\oplus W_2\oplus\dots\oplus W_k .V=W1​⊕W2​⊕⋯⊕Wk​.

remarkWhat the hypotheses buyOpen the observation

Both hypotheses of Maschke’s theorem earn their keep. Drop finiteness of the group and Example Reducible but indecomposable breaks the conclusion. Drop the field assumption and it breaks too: over fields of prime characteristic ppp dividing ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣, the averaging factor 1/∣G∣1/\abs G1/∣G∣ does not exist and complete reducibility fails (the subject called modular representation theory lives in the wreckage). Over R\RR, by contrast, the proof goes through verbatim with real inner products — though, as Example A representation that splits over C\CC but not over R\RR showed, the irreducible pieces over R\RR and over C\CC can differ. In Part II, compactness of the Lie group will play the role finiteness plays here.
